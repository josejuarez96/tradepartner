## Answer
**Verdict:** MIXED  ·  **Confidence:** medium on the grade, low on any point estimate of magnitude

The claim graded is: *a long-only, monthly 12-1 momentum portfolio of liquid US large/mid caps has earned a positive return over the market, net of costs, since 2010.*

- **No Tier 1 study was found that reports this quantity directly.** Specifically, none gave a post-2010, long-only, net-of-cost, large/mid-cap figure.
- **The closest live evidence shows the premium near zero or negative relative to the market:**
  - AQR's Large Cap Momentum Style Fund returned 13.22%/yr from July 2009 to December 2018, net of fees and real trading costs. The Russell 1000 returned 14.04%/yr over the same period.
  - MTUM's Tier 1 record gives absolute returns only (16.79%/yr NAV since April 2013). Its margin over the market could not be verified from a Tier 1/2 source in this budget, and its methodology is not monthly 12-1.
- **Academic evidence of weakening is consistent across the sources found:**
  - Momentum profits became insignificant from the late 1990s through 2012.
  - Anomaly returns net of costs since the early 2000s average a few basis points a month.
  - Mutual funds capture "no returns to momentum" after implementation costs.
  - Post-publication returns are 58% lower across 97 predictors.
- **Supporting evidence exists** (long legs carry about half of momentum profits; institutional trading costs are low). None of it is post-2010, long-only, and net of costs at the same time.

The evidence conflicts, and costs plausibly consume the premium for large-cap long-only implementations. That meets the handoff §6.1 MIXED definition. It does not reach NOT SUPPORTED, because no credible replication shows the long-only large-cap effect is *gone*. Live funds roughly tracked the market rather than falling clearly below it.

## Disconfirmation
Searches run that were aimed at evidence **against** a post-2010 long-only net premium, or against optimistic cost assumptions:
1. "momentum anomaly weakened after 2010 US equities decline … SSRN" → Ben-David et al. (NBER w28624); Bhattacharya et al. (RQFA 2017).
2. "Chen Velikov 'Zeroing in on the expected returns of anomalies' net returns post-2005" → average anomaly ~4 bp/mo net (JFQA abstract).
3. "Patton Weller 'What you see is not what you get' … momentum implementation gap" → mutual funds earn no momentum after costs.
4. "AQR Large Cap Momentum Style Fund … since inception … Russell 1000" plus two SEC-restricted searches → live fund trailed its benchmark from 2009 to 2018.
5. "McLean Pontiff … 26% out-of-sample 58% post-publication" → confirmed decay magnitudes (abstract).
6. "Hwang Rubesam 'Has momentum lost its momentum?'" → found Bhattacharya, Li & Sonaer instead (the title is theirs).
7. "critique momentum trading cost estimates Frazzini Israel Moskowitz too low …" → Patton & Weller argue that implementation-shortfall data from one institution is endogenous and biased downward. They treat it as "an informative lower bound" (summary of the paper's argument; the full text was not rendered).
8. "momentum ETF MTUM underperformed S&P 500 since 2013 …" → Advisor Perspectives (2023) extract: outperformance gone by 2023 (UNVERIFIED).
9. "Ben-David … Morningstar 2002 … momentum decline" → figures from search extracts only (see UNVERIFIED).

What was found against the claim:
- A live long-only large-cap momentum fund trailed the Russell 1000 by about 0.8 pp/yr over its first 9.5 years, after costs (Tier 1).
- Mutual funds capture no momentum after implementation costs (Tier 1). Institutional low-cost estimates are argued to be a lower bound.
- Long-short momentum has been insignificant since the late 1990s (to 2012), and anomaly net returns in the modern era are a few bp/month (Tier 1).
- A dated institutional mechanism, the 2002 Morningstar reform, is linked to the US decline (Tier 1 working paper).

What was found for the claim:
- MTUM's absolute returns are high (16.79%/yr since 2013). Its beta is 1.28 and it holds 54% in information technology. Market-relative outperformance could not be verified from Tier 1/2 in budget.
- Institutional trading costs appear low enough for momentum to survive at scale (FIM, 1998–2011).
- The long leg carries about half of momentum profits (Israel & Moskowitz).

## Implications for the Phase 3 spec (inputs for the owner, not decisions)
These are candidate numbers taken from the sources above, for the owner to accept, change or reject when writing the Phase 3 pre-registration.

1. **Prior for expected net excess return over the benchmark:** centred near **0 pp/yr**, plausibly **−1 to +1 pp/yr**.
   - The only Tier 1 live long-only large-cap figure is −0.82 pp/yr (2009–2018).
   - Academic net anomaly returns in the modern era are ~4 bp/month (~0.5%/yr) for long-short.
   - This is an input for the "expected magnitude" field, not a forecast.
2. **Backtest red-flag threshold (candidate):** treat a 2010+ backtest showing net excess over SPY of more than **~+3 pp/yr** as a trigger for a look-ahead or cost-model audit before anyone reads it as evidence. Such a result would exceed every live implementation found here. The +3 is the agent's framing of the gap, not a sourced number.
3. **Drawdown expectations for pre-set risk rules:**
   - Worst live quarter: **−18.19%** (AQR fund, 3Q11).
   - Worst live calendar year: **−18.23%** (MTUM, 2022).
   - Long-short crash benchmark: **−45.60%** (2009).
   - No Tier 1/2 *maximum* drawdown for a long-only implementation since 2010 was retrieved. A stop threshold set tighter than about −20% absolute would, on this evidence, likely have been hit at least once since 2010.
4. **Cost-model range for sensitivity runs:**
   - Low end: institutional-style costs (FIM, lower bound).
   - High end: large enough to erase momentum for a typical fund (Patton & Weller).
   - Live large-cap momentum turnover of about **66%/yr** (AQR fund, FY2018) is a sanity check for the backtest's turnover. A materially higher figure suggests the strategy trades more than the live implementation did.
5. **Detectability:** with an expected excess near zero, the handoff's D1 rule (t ≈ SR × √years) implies the backtest cannot distinguish the strategy from its benchmark. This is consistent with ADR 0005, which already treats benchmark-beating as optional. Whether to proceed on that basis is the owner's decision.

## Caveats & gaps
- **Design mismatch:** neither live fund is a monthly 12-1 portfolio. AQR used multiple momentum measures and a Russell 1000 universe; MTUM uses a risk-adjusted index. The MVP's equal-weight or top-N design and $5 / $5M filters may behave differently.
- **Survivor bias in the benchmark set:** the AQR fund's strategy was replaced in May 2026 by a multi-style fund, and its record after 2018 was only partly retrieved. MTUM is the survivor among momentum ETFs.
- **Full texts not read:** the PDFs could not be rendered in this environment (no poppler). Academic figures come from abstracts, so momentum-specific table values (e.g., Novy-Marx & Velikov RFS 2016 Table 5; the Chen & Velikov momentum row) were not read.
- **Pre-2010 samples:** most academic samples end between 2011 and 2013, so post-2010 academic coverage is thin. The post-2013 period rests on live products.
- **Exposure contamination:** MTUM's returns combine momentum with high beta (1.28) and heavy IT concentration (54%), so its absolute performance is not a clean momentum premium.
- **Costs and taxes:** fund returns exclude a retail investor's commissions or spread, and all taxes. Monthly turnover generates short-term gains (handoff D5).
- **Chen & Velikov figure:** the JFQA abstract says 4 bp/month. A search extract of an earlier version said 8 bp; the published figure is used.
